Stop Treating AI as an Operator

John Terrill · LinkedIn · /writing/stop-treating-ai-as-an-operator

The easiest way to misunderstand AI in infrastructure is to imagine it as a better operator.

Give it credentials.

Give it logs.

Give it access to production.

Let it make changes faster than a human.

That framing is tempting because it maps AI onto an existing role.

But I think it is the wrong starting point.

AI should not primarily be treated as an operator.

It should be treated as a participant in the engineering change process.

Operators Execute Known Procedures

Operators are essential.

They run playbooks.

They respond to incidents.

They apply changes.

They maintain systems.

Some of that work can and should be automated.

But consequential infrastructure change is not merely operation.

It involves judgment.

Before changing production, someone must answer questions like:

  • What is the intent?
  • What assumptions are we making?
  • What constraints matter?
  • What evidence supports the change?
  • What is the blast radius?
  • What would success look like?
  • What happens if we are wrong?

Those questions are not operational details.

They are the reasoning that makes operation safe.

AI Should Strengthen Reasoning Before Execution

If an AI proposes a firewall change, the interesting question is not whether it can generate the command.

It probably can.

The interesting question is whether it can help the organization understand the change well enough to approve it.

That means surfacing:

  • affected systems
  • dependent services
  • historical incidents
  • similar prior changes
  • rollback strategy
  • validation steps
  • confidence level
  • evidence gaps

This is where AI can become incredibly useful.

Not by bypassing the process.

By improving the process.

The Operator Model Encourages Too Much Trust

When we treat AI as an operator, the main safety question becomes:

> How much access should it have?

That pushes the discussion toward permissions, ACLs, and guardrails.

Those matter.

But they are not enough.

A human with excessive access can still make a bad change.

An AI with limited access can still recommend a bad change.

The deeper issue is not access.

It is whether the proposed action is embedded in a disciplined reasoning workflow.

The Better Model Is Structured Change

A better model looks like this:

AI can propose.

AI can analyze.

AI can compare alternatives.

AI can identify missing assumptions.

AI can generate rollback plans.

AI can summarize evidence.

But consequential changes still move through structured review.

The system preserves:

  • what was proposed
  • why it was proposed
  • what evidence supported it
  • what risks were identified
  • who approved it
  • what happened afterward

That turns AI from an opaque operator into an auditable engineering participant.

This Helps Humans Too

The most interesting part is that this is not an AI-specific standard.

Human engineers should also propose changes this way.

If an engineer cannot explain why a production change is safe, the organization should not accept the change merely because the engineer is human.

AI exposes a discipline that should have existed already.

The future of safe infrastructure is not special rules for AI.

It is better engineering rules for everyone.

The Real Shift

We should stop asking only:

> Can AI perform the task?

We should ask:

> Can AI improve the quality of the reasoning around the task?

That is a different standard.

It makes AI valuable before it ever touches production.

It also keeps humans in the right role.

Not as rubber-stamp approvers.

As accountable participants in a reasoning process that becomes stronger because AI is involved.

The Point

The safest AI infrastructure systems will not be the ones that simply give AI root access with better guardrails.

They will be the ones that make every proposed change understandable, reviewable, observable, and reversible.

AI does not need to become the operator of record.

It needs to become a better participant in engineering judgment.

That is a much higher bar.

And it is the one worth building toward.
