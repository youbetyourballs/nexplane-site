Why Infrastructure Needs a Control Plane

John Terrill · LinkedIn · /writing/why-infrastructure-needs-a-control-plane

Modern enterprises are well-equipped to observe their infrastructure. Dashboards, metrics, logs, traces, alerting — the observability stack has matured considerably over the last decade. What enterprises are not well-equipped to do is change their infrastructure safely. The tooling for observation and the tooling for change have developed independently, and the gap between them is where most operational risk actually lives. Watching something carefully is not the same as being able to act on what you see without creating a new problem.

Change today is scattered. A firewall rule change goes through one tool. An IAM policy goes through another. A DNS update goes through a third. A certificate rotation involves a manual process that someone documented once and that no one has tested since. A software update on a fleet of servers requires a script that lives in someone's home directory. Each of these changes has its own approval path, its own audit trail, and its own rollback story — or more often, no rollback story at all. The organization operates not as a coordinated system but as a collection of independent change mechanisms that have never been unified.

A control plane coordinates what the current stack leaves fragmented. It provides a single place where change intent is expressed, dependencies are checked, blast radius is estimated, approvals are recorded, execution is tracked, verification runs, and rollback is available on demand. That is not a dashboard. It is the connective tissue between the humans who make decisions and the systems that carry them out. Without it, coordination happens through Slack threads and institutional memory, both of which are unreliable and neither of which scales.

The fragmentation is expensive in ways that show up clearly after incidents and less clearly during normal operations. Postmortems become reconstructions because the change record is scattered across five systems. Remediation slows because the team cannot quickly determine what changed, in what order, with what dependencies. Handoffs between teams break because each team's change workflow is different. None of this is dramatic. It is a steady tax on every engineering organization that has grown beyond the point where one person can hold the whole system in their head.

AI makes this problem more urgent, not less. An AI agent with infrastructure access and no structured environment to operate within is not an assistant. It is a source of fast, confident, hard-to-audit changes. The value of AI in infrastructure work is proportional to the quality of the control plane surrounding it. With a control plane — with typed change requests, dependency graphs, approval gates, rollback requirements, and verification signals — AI can do genuinely useful work. It can draft plans, flag risk, propose rollbacks, and learn from outcomes. Without a control plane, it can only move fast in an environment where moving fast without structure is precisely the problem. The next decade of infrastructure will be won by systems that can coordinate change. Dashboards alone will not be enough.
