Fear vs. Risk

John Terrill · LinkedIn · /writing/fear-vs-risk

Organizations often confuse fear with risk, and the confusion is costly. Risk is an estimate — what can go wrong, how likely it is, what it would cost. Fear is something different. Fear is what happens when teams cannot see a safe path forward. When the blast radius is unclear. When rollback is not guaranteed. When the last time someone made a similar change it took three days to recover. Fear is not irrational, but it is not the same as risk, and treating them as interchangeable leads to bad decisions in both directions.

When fear drives decisions, organizations tend to freeze on the things that are genuinely risky and also freeze on the things that only feel risky. The deferred patch that has been sitting on the backlog for eight months is not there because the team calculated the risk and concluded inaction was correct. It is there because someone once tried a similar patch and it caused an outage, and no one has built a safer way to make the change. Fear is not a risk calculation. It is a response to the absence of a safe path.

When risk drives decisions, the picture looks different. Teams can say: here is the probability of something going wrong, here is the impact if it does, here is the rollback plan, here is what verification looks like. They can make a reasoned decision about whether to proceed. Sometimes the answer is still no — but it is a reasoned no, not a reflexive one. And the team can have that conversation explicitly instead of letting the change quietly age on a backlog.

A system with good rollback, simulation, staged rollout, and verification does something important: it reduces fear without pretending risk is zero. It makes the path forward visible. It gives teams the information they need to make a real decision instead of a fear-based one. That is not the same as eliminating risk — it is converting vague fear into concrete, manageable risk. And that conversion is worth a lot.

Nexplane is designed around this distinction. The goal is not to make infrastructure change feel safe when it is not. The goal is to give teams the tools to see risk clearly, plan against it, and move forward with confidence when the plan is sound. Make the path visible. Make the rollback real. Make the decision rational. That is what it means to convert fear into risk.
