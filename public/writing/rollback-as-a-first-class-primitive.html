Rollback as a First-Class Primitive

John Terrill · LinkedIn · /writing/rollback-as-a-first-class-primitive

Rollback is usually treated as an emergency procedure. Something goes wrong, an incident fires, and someone starts frantically tracing through what changed and whether it can be reversed. That is the wrong order of operations. Rollback should be designed before execution begins, not improvised after something breaks. If a system can execute a change but cannot articulate how to reverse it, that system is incomplete. It has half an answer.

I have watched teams deploy changes they genuinely could not roll back. Not because they were reckless, but because rollback was never part of the design conversation. The change was scoped, reviewed, approved, and executed. The rollback path was assumed. Then something went sideways and the assumption collapsed. What followed was hours of careful work trying to reconstruct a safe state from a system that had already moved. That is avoidable, but only if rollback is treated as a requirement rather than an afterthought.

Rollback is not a sign of failure. Rollback is resilience. The ability to undo is what makes bold changes survivable. Teams that can roll back quickly can ship more aggressively because the cost of being wrong is bounded. Teams that cannot roll back ship conservatively, accumulate risk slowly, and then experience the failure they were trying to avoid anyway, just at a worse time and at larger scale.

For security work, this matters more than in most domains. A firewall rule, a network segmentation policy, an identity change, a certificate rotation, a DNS update — any of these can be correct in theory and disruptive in practice. Security controls touch production behavior. When a control is applied and something breaks unexpectedly, the team needs a clear path back. Not a guess. Not a reconstruction from notes. A designed, tested, executable reversal. That is what it means to know the way home.

The practical implication is that rollback design should happen at change authoring time, not at incident time. Before a change is approved, the question should already be answered: if this goes wrong at step three, what exactly do we do? That question disciplines the planning. It surfaces assumptions. It often catches complexity that would have been invisible until production. A control plane that makes rollback a required field rather than an optional footnote changes the quality of the work that flows through it.
