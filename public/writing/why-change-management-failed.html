Why Change Management Failed

John Terrill · LinkedIn · /writing/why-change-management-failed

Traditional change management started with a reasonable idea: production systems are risky to change, so we should be deliberate about it. Review the change before it happens. Understand the impact. Know who is responsible. Have a plan if something goes wrong. That is sound thinking. The problem is that the tooling and process built to implement it drifted steadily away from engineering reality, until what remained was process for the sake of process and paperwork for the sake of audit. The artifact replaced the outcome.

I have worked in organizations where the change advisory board met weekly and reviewed slides. Engineers filled out forms to describe work that was already done or already scheduled to happen regardless of what the board said. The board asked questions in a language that was one level of abstraction removed from how the systems actually worked. Approvals happened based on the quality of the presentation, not the quality of the engineering. Everyone knew this was theater, and everyone participated in it anyway because the alternative was a compliance finding.

The failure was not in wanting control. The failure was in separating control from engineering reality. When the change management system does not connect to the systems being changed, it cannot provide real oversight. It can only provide the appearance of oversight. That appearance is expensive. It consumes engineering time, slows legitimate work, and creates friction that encourages teams to route around the process whenever they can. The actual risk does not go away. It just moves off the record.

The next generation of change management should not be more forms. It should be executable change intelligence. Plans connected to execution. Approvals connected to the actual scope of the change, not a description of it. Rollback designed before the first action runs, not reconstructed after something breaks. Verification built into the workflow so the system knows whether the change achieved its intent. History that surfaces patterns and improves future decisions. These are not utopian goals. They are the natural result of building a control plane that is actually connected to the infrastructure it governs.

The engineers I have worked with who hated change management did not hate the idea of being careful with production systems. They hated the overhead of a process that was disconnected from the work. Give those same engineers tooling that makes the change itself the record — that makes planning cheaper, approval faster, rollback guaranteed, and verification automatic — and the resistance disappears. The goal was always right. The execution was the problem.
